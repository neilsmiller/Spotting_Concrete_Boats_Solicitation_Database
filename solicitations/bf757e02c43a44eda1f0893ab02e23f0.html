Sin Analysis

Compliance Over Outcomes
Does the contract measure process compliance rather than actual outcomes?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation exhibits a moderate compliance-over-outcomes problem. On one hand, it is meaningfully better than a pure compliance document: Section A.4 establishes concrete recruitment targets (200 leads, 16 applications per new school, 15% year-over-year growth, 40 recruitment events, 72 office hours), and payment is partly linked to actual recruiter hours worked (A.6.1(A) and A.8). These are genuine outcome-oriented elements that prevent a severity-3 rating. On the other hand, the payment-triggering performance measures in A.6.1 are dominated by process compliance: was a recruiter hired, are office hours posted, are reports submitted on time, was training attended. The outcome targets from A.4 do not appear in the payment or option-year approval sections with any enforcement mechanism — they exist as plan elements subject to Peace Corps approval, but missing them does not appear to reduce payment or block option exercise. The three-layered reporting cadence (monthly, mid-year, final) with government-prescribed formats delivered annually creates a structural emphasis on documentation compliance. The option-year approval criteria in A.7 further cement this pattern: three of four criteria are process measures, and the results criterion is framed relative to an internally approved plan rather than against independent standards. The net effect is a bifurcated structure: outcome targets exist on paper in the strategy plan but are not the primary accountability mechanism, while process compliance — submitting reports, attending training, hiring on schedule — drives both payment and contract continuation. This is a moderate manifestation of the sin, not severe, because the outcome targets do exist and the staffing-hours payment formula does tie payment to actual service delivery. Severity 2 is appropriate.
Evidence Quotes
The Strat Recruiter has: Taken the mandatory Strat Training(s) / Submitted Recruitment Plan(s) in the format and by the deadline provided by Peace Corps for review and approval / Submitted Monthly Reports in format and by the deadline provided by Peace Corps / Submitted a Mid-Year Report in format and by the deadline provided by Peace Corps / Submitted a Final Year-End Report in format and by the deadline provided by Peace Corps.
Section A.6.1(G) lists the payment-linked performance measurements exclusively as process compliance and submission activities — training attendance, plan submission, and report submissions on schedule. None of these payment triggers ask whether recruitment actually improved, whether leads converted to applicants, or whether the campus program met its targets. Payment is conditioned on following the process, not on producing outcomes.
Monthly Report: The Contractor shall ensure the Campus Recruiter submits monthly progress reports to the designated Peace Corps representative... Mid-Year Report: Contractor shall submit a mid-year report to the designated Peace Corps contact by the 2nd of January... Final Report: the Contractor shall submit a final report to the designated Peace Corps contact within 2 weeks of the last day of Spring Finals.
Three distinct reporting cadences are required — monthly, mid-year, and final — each with prescribed formats to be provided by Peace Corps annually. This is a layered reporting structure where the act of submitting reports in the right format by the right deadline is itself the compliance mechanism. The mid-year and final reports (Attachment C) focus on narrative self-assessment of activities and strategies rather than measured performance against defined targets.
(A) Quality of the approved/final Recruitment Plan(s)... (B) Overall execution and results in relation to the Recruitment Plan approved by the Peace Corps (C) Complete monthly, mid-year, and final reports submitted by the deadlines... (D) Strat and/or University point of contact attendance of assigned Peace Corps training
Section A.7 lists the option-year approval criteria. Three of the four criteria are process-compliance measures: plan quality, report submission timeliness, and training attendance. Criterion (B) references 'results in relation to the Recruitment Plan' but the plan itself is approved by Peace Corps and sets targets that are not defined in the contract — making this a plan-adherence measure rather than an independent outcome standard. The actual recruitment outcome metrics (leads, applications, conversion rates) are not enumerated as option-year triggers.
Generate a minimum total of 200 new leads throughout the year. / Generate a minimum of 16 applications for each new school, and produce 15% more applications year over year for all returning Strat schools.
These are genuine outcome metrics — specific, measurable targets for leads and applications — which represent meaningful outcome orientation. However, they appear in the Recruitment Strategy Plan section (A.4) as targets 'that will be shared with the school during training,' not as contractually enforceable performance standards. They are not listed among the payment-linked performance measurements in A.6.1 or the option-year criteria in A.7, which weakens their accountability function and leaves compliance-focused reporting as the dominant accountability mechanism.
The actual monthly report format to be used throughout the performance period will be provided to the Campus Recruiter at the beginning of every academic year... The mid-year report format and required information will be provided to the Campus Recruiter at the beginning of every academic year... The final report format and required information will be provided to the Campus Recruiter at the beginning of every academic year.
Report formats are deferred to post-award guidance rather than defined in the contract. This means the government retains discretion to define what 'compliance' looks like after award, and the contractor's accountability is structured around satisfying government-prescribed formats. This reinforces a compliance orientation: contractors demonstrate acceptable performance by filling out the right form correctly, not by hitting independently verifiable outcome targets.
Insider Rewards
Do requirements or criteria favor incumbent or well-connected contractors?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation presents a moderate insider rewards problem driven primarily by one unusually direct mechanism: explicit incumbency as an eligibility criterion. The 'previously awarded a Strat contract' carve-out is not a scoring advantage or a past performance preference — it is a threshold requirement for participation. This is structurally equivalent to a sole-source justification for the incumbent pool, embedded within an ostensibly competitive solicitation. Any university that has held a Strat contract, regardless of its current size or capability, can compete; any university that has not, regardless of capability, must meet the enrollment threshold. Mitigating factors prevent a severity-3 rating. First, the enrollment threshold (now 10,000 undergraduates after Amendment 0001) is a substantial pool — hundreds of U.S. institutions qualify on size alone. Second, the nature of this contract is genuinely institution-specific: the contractor must be the university itself, providing physical space, HR infrastructure, and campus access. There is no market of commercial third-party vendors being excluded; the competition is among universities. Third, the government is awarding up to 40 contracts across 8 regions, with 1–8 awards per region, suggesting genuine multi-vendor intent. Fourth, the skills tested are not deeply technical — campus recruiting is a well-understood function that any large university career services operation can perform. Nevertheless, the explicit incumbency track in the eligibility criteria, reinforced by past performance evaluation language that references 'past work done for Peace Corps,' creates a two-tiered competition in which prior Strat holders have structural advantages unavailable to comparably capable new entrants. The original 20,000-undergraduate threshold that had to be amended downward further suggests that the initial drafting was calibrated to protect the incumbent pool. Severity 2 is appropriate: the playing field is meaningfully tilted toward incumbents, but the broad enrollment-based eligibility track ensures genuine competition remains open to a large class of new entrants.
Evidence Quotes
This procurement is open to U.S. higher education institutions that meet at least one (1) of the following eligibility criteria: - United States higher education institutions with at least 20,000 undergraduate students; or - Institutions previously awarded a Strat contract with the Peace Corps.
The eligibility criteria create a two-track system: large institutions (originally 20,000+ undergraduates) OR prior Strat contract holders. The second track explicitly preserves access for incumbents regardless of size, while the first track uses enrollment as a proxy for recruitment capacity. This was the original formulation before Amendment 0001 lowered the threshold to 10,000.
The purpose of this amendment is to revise the eligibility criteria in Section 2.1 and Section 6.1 regarding undergraduate students from 20,000 to 10,000.
The original 20,000-undergraduate threshold was apparently restrictive enough that the government needed to amend it downward. This suggests the original threshold functioned as a de facto incumbent-protection mechanism — the prior Strat schools that didn't meet 20,000 undergraduates were at risk of exclusion, prompting a correction. The amendment is a mitigating factor (broadening competition) but also reveals that the initial drafting was exclusionary.
Institutions previously awarded a Strat contract with the Peace Corps.
This is an explicit incumbency carve-out embedded in the eligibility criteria — not past performance scoring, but a threshold requirement for participation. Any institution that previously held a Strat contract is automatically eligible regardless of size, ensuring no incumbent is excluded. This is the clearest form of insider reward: prior contract holders receive guaranteed access to compete that is unavailable to equally capable institutions without prior Strat history.
Historically, larger institutions and prior Strat participants have demonstrated stronger recruitment performance outcomes, including higher lead and applicant volume, stronger conversion rates, and greater alignment with Peace Corps' recruitment goals.
This justification for the eligibility structure explicitly advantages prior Strat participants as a class, using observed incumbency performance to rationalize structural preference. While the logic has surface validity, it conflates correlation with causation — prior participants may perform better partly because they have already built infrastructure, relationships, and institutional familiarity that new entrants would develop over time.
Offeror's past performance on the extent to which the offeror demonstrates successful past performance in the areas described in the Performance Work Statement. In evaluating past performance, the Government may contact the references provided by the offeror and other sources of information, including past work done for Peace Corps.
The past performance evaluation specifically calls out 'past work done for Peace Corps' as an information source the Government may consult. This signals that Peace Corps-specific prior work is valued in evaluation, giving prior Strat holders an inherent scoring advantage over equally capable institutions without Peace Corps contract history. Combined with the eligibility criteria that explicitly name prior Strat holders, this stacks a second layer of incumbent advantage into evaluation scoring.
Requirement Sprawl
Does the scope dictate HOW work is done rather than WHAT must be achieved?
Moderate 4 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation exhibits moderate requirement sprawl (Severity 2). The Peace Corps Strat program has a legitimate and well-defined mission — recruiting volunteers through campus-based staff — and the solicitation does include genuine outcome metrics: lead and application targets, conversion rates, and year-over-year growth expectations. These are properly outcome-oriented. However, substantial portions of the Performance Work Statement dictate implementation in ways that constrain contractor innovation. The physical office setup requirements specify exact equipment and software. The staffing model mandates a precise structure (one part-time RPCV recruiter at 20 hours/week plus one institutional supervisor). Activity counts (40 events, 72 hours of office hours, 20 partnership connections) prescribe how recruitment is achieved, not just what must be achieved. These read as the operational practices of a prior incumbent codified into contract requirements rather than derived from an assessment of what outcomes the government actually needs. Mitigating factors: this is not a severe case. The solicitation is short and coherent; it does not approach the volume of prescriptive process mandates that would warrant a severity 3 rating. The outcome metrics (leads, applications) are present and meaningful. The RPCV requirement has a defensible programmatic rationale. The prescriptive elements are concentrated in the PWS setup and staffing sections and do not dominate the entire document. A vendor bidding on this RFQ would feel constrained in how to organize and staff the work, but the core recruitment outcomes are clearly defined and measurable. Severity 2 is appropriate.
Evidence Quotes
The Contractor shall establish a Peace Corps Campus Recruiting space on campus and provide access to the following campus resources: Physical office space with Peace Corps branded signage located in an area that is accessible and highly visible to students. Convenient private space must be easily accessible for the Campus Recruiter to conduct confidential meetings. The Contractor will furnish a bulletin board or some other form of highly visible posting area, a designated telephone (with some form of voice-messaging service), a computer with MS Office Suite software and access to the Internet, office supplies, and access to copier. The Campus Recruiter is forbidden to use a personally owned computer for Peace Corps business.
This passage prescribes the physical setup of a recruiting office in granular detail — specifying the signage, bulletin board, telephone with voicemail, specific software suite (MS Office), and even prohibiting personal computers. These are process and equipment mandates, not outcome requirements. The government's actual need is that students can access a recruiter and that information can be communicated; the specific furnishings and equipment configuration are implementation choices the contractor (university) should be free to determine. Requiring MS Office Suite specifically, for example, forecloses alternatives that might be equally or more effective.
The Campus Recruiter must be a Returned Peace Corps Volunteer (RPCV), who is approved by the Peace Corps and is to be paid a fair and reasonable hourly rate plus the fringe benefits the Contractor typically offers its part-time employees or graduate students... The Contractor shall assign an institutional employee to work as the supervisor for the Campus Recruiter.
This requirement prescribes not only the staffing structure (a specific part-time campus recruiter plus an institutional supervisor) but also the credential of that specific person (RPCV status). While the RPCV requirement has a plausible programmatic rationale, the combined prescription of who must be hired, at what hours (20 hours/week), what credential they must hold, and the supervisory structure removes contractor flexibility to organize the work differently — e.g., using multiple part-time staff, a different supervision model, or innovative approaches to campus engagement.
Conduct 40 unique recruitment events... Conduct public office hours — Minimum of 2 hours per week for a total minimum of 72 hours per year... Conduct 20 unique partnership connections... Generate a minimum total of 200 new leads throughout the year... Generate a minimum of 16 applications for each new school
This section mixes legitimate outcome metrics (leads, applications) with prescriptive activity counts (40 events, 2 hours/week office hours, 20 partnership connections). The activity counts dictate HOW the contractor achieves recruitment — mandating a specific number of events and office hours is a process prescription, not an outcome. A contractor might achieve better results with fewer, higher-quality events or different engagement strategies, but is locked into hitting these activity targets regardless. The application and lead minimums are legitimate outcome measures; the event and office hour counts are sprawl.
Monthly Report: The Contractor shall ensure the Campus Recruiter submits monthly progress reports to the designated Peace Corps representative... Mid-Year Report: Contractor shall submit a mid-year report... Final Report: the Contractor shall submit a final report... The final report format and required information will be provided to the Campus Recruiter at the beginning of every academic year.
Four separate mandatory reports (monthly, mid-year, final, and recruitment plan) with prescribed formats to be defined by Peace Corps at the start of each year represents a significant reporting burden. While some reporting is legitimate, the monthly cadence plus mid-year plus final reports — with formats dictated by the government rather than the contractor — reflects a process-control orientation. More importantly, the formats are deferred to Peace Corps discretion annually, meaning contractors cannot fully evaluate compliance burden at solicitation time. This is a mild but real sprawl element.
Rule Pools
Does the contract bury contractors in undifferentiated regulatory citations?
Minimal 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation does not exhibit a meaningful rule pool problem. The regulatory citations are predominantly standard FAR Part 12 commercial services clauses incorporated by reference — a normal and appropriate mechanism for federal contracting. The solicitation does not include a dedicated 'Applicable Documents' section with a long enumerated list of agency policies, directives, executive orders, or external standards. The D.6 Executive Orders section references a single DEI executive order with enough specificity to indicate intent. The Section 508 provision is a standard DOI addition. The sustainable products clause is mildly questionable in context (campus recruitment services do not obviously involve procuring physical products subject to EPA/USDA sustainable purchasing requirements), but it is a single clause with self-limiting language, not evidence of an accumulated, uncurated list. A vendor reading this solicitation would face a manageable and largely predictable compliance landscape. The regulatory burden is appropriate for the scope and contract type. Severity 1 is warranted.

Square Peg
Is the pricing/contract type mismatched to the nature of the work?
Minimal 4 quotes ▾
Reasoning
FFP is appropriate here. The core deliverable is a recurring, well-defined service: a university hosts a Peace Corps campus recruiting office, hires a known part-time employee (20 hours/week), conducts a specified number of events (40 per year), maintains office hours (minimum 72 hours/year), and submits standardized reports. The labor quantity and type are predictable, and the government has even structured a pro-rata payment formula for partial performance that further de-risks the FFP structure. The requirement is essentially a staffed service with defined outputs — not open-ended research or complex IT development where scope is uncertain. There is no evidence that the work will expand unpredictably or that requirements will evolve mid-contract in ways that would make cost estimation unreliable. T&M would be inappropriate here because the labor quantity is specified and the government wants output accountability (leads, applications, events), not just billable hours. Cost-reimbursement would be excessive overhead for what is essentially a straightforward recurring service. The FFP structure, combined with the pro-rata reduction mechanism, creates appropriate incentive alignment: the university bears the risk of recruiter hiring and retention, while the government pays only for actual recruiter performance. This is a textbook appropriate use of FFP for a commercial service with predictable scope.

The core requirement is a well-defined recurring service: each university provides office space, hires a part-time campus recruiter (~20 hours/week), and executes a defined recruitment plan. The scope is stable and predictable year over year, making FFP appropriate.

Performance targets are clearly quantified and tied to defined deliverables, which supports the use of FFP. The requirements are specific enough that a vendor can estimate costs with reasonable accuracy.
