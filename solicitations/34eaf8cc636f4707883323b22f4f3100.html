Sin Analysis

Compliance Over Outcomes
Does the contract measure process compliance rather than actual outcomes?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation exhibits a moderate compliance-over-outcomes problem. On one hand, the contract is for a straightforward utility service (cable TV and internet) with a single responsible source, and the administrative burden is light — there are no multiple overlapping report types, no prescribed report formats with mandated sections, and no excessive meeting cadences. The solicitation does not exhibit the most severe form of this sin. On the other hand, the Performance Requirements Summary — the document's primary accountability mechanism — is structurally deficient as an outcome measurement tool. The 95% AQL figures are attached to performance requirements that simply cross-reference a paragraph describing government-furnished property and locations, not service quality standards. There are no defined metrics for uptime, channel availability, internet throughput, mean time to restore, or user satisfaction. The surveillance method (annual random inspection) checks whether service exists at a point in time rather than tracking whether it performed reliably over the contract period. The QA apparatus confirms the process is running, not that the service is good. The one genuine performance-adjacent requirement — the 2-to-4-hour outage response time — measures process response rather than service restoration outcomes. It is a meaningful constraint but insufficient alone to shift the accountability structure toward outcome orientation. The CDR/CAP process reinforces the compliance frame by triggering on failures of the 'quality control system, personnel, instructions, controls, tests, or records' — all internal process elements — rather than on service degradation experienced by patients and families. Severity 2 is appropriate: the solicitation has some outcome-adjacent elements (the AQL percentages and the response-time requirement) and lacks the severe multi-layered reporting burden that would warrant severity 3, but the core QA mechanism is compliance-referential and the most important outcome metrics for a utility service — availability, reliability, and user experience — are entirely absent.
Evidence Quotes
Performance Element: Cable Service (TV) | Performance Requirement: IAW PWS Paragraph_3.0__ | Surveillance Method: Random Inspection | Frequency: Annual | Acceptable Quality Level: 95% Internet Service | IAW PWS Paragraph 3.0____ | Random Inspection | Annual | 95%
The Performance Requirements Summary (PRS) defines quality level numerically (95%) but the 'Performance Requirement' field is simply a cross-reference to paragraph 3.0, which describes Government Furnished Property and locations — not actual service performance standards. There are no defined metrics for what constitutes acceptable cable or internet service (e.g., uptime percentage, channel availability, throughput speeds, mean time to restore). The 95% AQL is a number without a measurable outcome attached to it, making it a compliance threshold rather than a genuine performance standard.
Surveillance Method: Random Inspection | Frequency: Annual
For both cable TV and internet services, the surveillance method is 'random inspection' conducted only annually. This approach checks whether service is present at a given moment rather than tracking cumulative service quality over time. There are no continuous monitoring mechanisms, user-reported satisfaction measures, or outage tracking metrics. Annual spot-checks of an ongoing utility service are compliance theater — they confirm the service exists on inspection day, not that it performed reliably across the year.
4.1 Inspection Requirements. The Contractor shall provide quality services and/or products. In accordance with (IAW) this contract. 4.2 Government surveillance of Contractor performance is not limited to the Performance Objectives as outlined in this Performance Requirements Summary (PRS). The Government reserves the right to conduct compliance surveillance of any contractual requirement of this acquisition.
Section 4.1 defines the quality standard as simply providing services 'in accordance with this contract' — a circular, process-referential standard. Section 4.2 explicitly reserves the right to conduct 'compliance surveillance' of contractual requirements, framing QA as a compliance-checking exercise. Neither section defines what good service looks like from the user's perspective or establishes outcome measures such as service availability rates or patient/patient-family satisfaction.
4.4 Emergent Services. The Contractor shall respond to all service outages, including individual unit failures and building-wide failures, within two (2) to four (4) hours of notification.
This is the one near-outcome measure in the document — a response time requirement. However, it measures process adherence (did the contractor show up within 4 hours?) rather than a service outcome (was the outage resolved within a defined period? what is the maximum allowable cumulative downtime per month or year?). Response time is a proxy metric that does not capture whether the service was actually restored or how frequently outages occur. It partially mitigates the compliance-over-outcomes problem but does not resolve it.
4.3 Contract Deficiency Reports (CDR). At any time, it is determined by the Contracting Officer and/or the Contracting Officer's Representative (COR) that the quality control system, personnel, instructions, controls, tests, or records are not providing results which conform to contract requirements, action shall be taken by the Contractor to correct the deficiency. If a CDR is issued the Contractor shall develop a Corrective Action Plan (CAP) which identifies the root cause, Corrective Action (CA) for the root cause, CA for the specific non-conformance and CA to the root cause to prevent recurrence and a corrective action including the timeline for completion.
The CDR/CAP mechanism triggers when the 'quality control system, personnel, instructions, controls, tests, or records' fail to conform — all process and documentation elements. The corrective action process itself is entirely process-focused (root cause documentation, corrective action plans, timelines for completion). There is no mention of service restoration benchmarks, user impact assessments, or outcome-based thresholds that would trigger a CDR. This confirms that the QA backstop is compliance-oriented.
Insider Rewards
Do requirements or criteria favor incumbent or well-connected contractors?
Minimal 0 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation is a sole-source notice posted under FAR 6.302-1 (only one responsible source), explicitly stating that the Government intends to award directly to Sparklight and is 'not seeking competitive proposals.' The sin of Insider Rewards — experience requirements that exclude non-incumbents — is structurally inapplicable here because there are no experience requirements, evaluation criteria, or past performance thresholds at all. The document contains no scoring rubric, no offeror qualification standards, and no capability or past performance evaluation framework. The reason no other vendor can compete is not that the solicitation has constructed insider-favoring evaluation criteria, but rather that Sparklight holds the geographic franchise and physical cable infrastructure serving Naval Hospital Beaufort and MCRD Parris Island. This is a natural monopoly situation driven by physical infrastructure and franchise agreements, not by solicitation design. The PWS requirements are straightforward operational standards (response times, safety compliance, employee conduct, base access) that any commercial cable provider would routinely meet. There is no stacking of dollar thresholds, federal-specific experience mandates, or institutional knowledge requirements because the agency is not evaluating competing vendors at all. The appropriate analytical lens here is whether the sole-source justification is legitimate — which is a separate question — not whether the solicitation's evaluation criteria favor the incumbent. For purposes of this sin specifically, severity 1 is appropriate: the document simply does not contain the type of experience requirements or evaluation criteria that could constitute insider rewards.
Requirement Sprawl
Does the scope dictate HOW work is done rather than WHAT must be achieved?
Minimal 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation exhibits minimal requirement sprawl. It is a straightforward sole-source utility contract for cable TV and internet services at fixed military installations — a commodity service with inherently limited scope for methodological variation. The PWS is largely structured around administrative and access requirements (base access, contractor identification, vehicle registration, OSHA compliance, English language requirements) that are either legal/security mandates or standard boilerplate for work performed on military installations. None of these prescribe HOW cable service must be technically delivered. The performance requirements summary defines outcomes at a high level (95% AQL for Cable TV and Internet Service, 100% for invoicing accuracy) with annual and monthly surveillance, which is outcome-oriented. The 2-4 hour outage response SLA is a measurable service level, not a process mandate. The document does contain some boilerplate language — large sections of administrative specification that appear copied from a standard DHA PWS template — but this is template reuse rather than sprawl in the meaningful sense. These sections govern contractor conduct and facility access on a military base, which are genuine constraints imposed by the security environment, not arbitrary process prescriptions. The most notable observation is that the PWS's performance requirements table references 'IAW PWS Paragraph 3.0' for both Cable TV and Internet Service, but Section 3.0 addresses Government Furnished Property rather than service standards. This is a documentation error, not sprawl. Overall, the solicitation appropriately defines what the government needs (cable TV and internet service at specific locations, specific unit counts, with reliable uptime and fast outage response) without prescribing the technical architecture, staffing model, or delivery method.
Evidence Quotes

This response time requirement apples during normal operating hours as well as for issues resported after normal business hours, unless otherwise directed by the Contracting Officer or COR. This is outcome-oriented: it specifies a response time SLA (2-4 hours) and an availability requirement (on-call capability) without dictating how the contractor must staff, route, or manage that on-call function. It defines WHAT is needed, not HOW to deliver it.

This is a standard all-inclusive responsibility clause common in utility/infrastructure service contracts. While it mentions specific equipment types (modems, cable boxes, wiring), these are illustrative examples of what the service entails rather than prescriptive mandates on method. The phrase 'such as but not limited to' preserves contractor discretion on actual implementation.
Rule Pools
Does the contract bury contractors in undifferentiated regulatory citations?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation presents a moderate rule pools problem. It is not a severe case: the contract is a standard utility services procurement under FAR Part 13 simplified acquisition procedures, the total clause count (~27 FAR/DFARS clauses plus a few PWS-cited standards) is not extreme by federal contracting standards, and many of the cited clauses (SAM registration, options, payment, equal opportunity) are genuinely applicable boilerplate. There are no absurd contradictions of the type seen in worst-case rule pool solicitations. However, several characteristics elevate this beyond minimal: First, the clause list is presented entirely without context — no explanation of how any clause applies to this specific work is provided anywhere in the solicitation. Second, multiple citations appear to be template carryovers poorly matched to the actual scope: NIST SP 800-171 cybersecurity assessment requirements for a cable TV utility provider, the Army Corps EM 385-1-1 construction safety manual for cable technicians, and F.O.B. Destination shipping terms for a services contract are the most notable. Third, the PWS itself (from the 'DHA PWS Template Under SAT August 2022 Rev. 1' template) contains administrative sections — holiday observance provisions, furlough guidance, language fluency requirements, vehicle identification requirements — that collectively add compliance surface area without clear operational necessity for what is essentially a utility bill. The sole-source nature of this award (Sparklight as the only cable provider with franchise agreements on these installations) means that competition impact is moot, which somewhat mitigates the practical harm from the rule pools problem. An incumbent monopoly provider already knows which of these clauses get enforced. Nevertheless, the absence of any explanatory context and the presence of plausibly irrelevant citations confirm a moderate severity finding.

The solicitation lists approximately 20+ FAR and DFARS clauses in undifferentiated table form with no contextual explanation of how each applies to a straightforward cable TV and internet utility contract. While many (SAM registration, options clauses, payment instructions) are standard boilerplate, several raise questions about relevance to this scope — e.g., 52.232-40 (Accelerated Payments to Small Business Subcontractors) for a sole-source award to a large cable company, and 52.247-34 (F.O.B. 252.204-7012 Safeguarding Covered Defense Information and Cyber Incident Reporting | 252.204-7019 Notice of NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Requirements | 252.204-7020 NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Requirements
Three cybersecurity/information assurance clauses — including NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Requirements — are cited with no explanation of their applicability to a cable TV and internet utility service contract. NIST SP 800-171 governs the protection of Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) in contractor information systems. A cable TV provider delivering commercial television signals and internet connectivity to a military hospital does not obviously handle CUI, making these citations appear to be template carryovers rather than curated requirements. No context is provided explaining what CUI the contractor would process or how 800-171 would be operationalized.
52.223-18 Encouraging Contractor Policies to Ban Text Messaging While Driving
This clause is cited in a solicitation for cable TV and internet utility services. While the contractor's technicians would drive vehicles on base (making some road safety requirements plausible), citing a full FAR clause specifically about text-messaging-while-driving policies — without explanation — in a utility services contract reads as boilerplate accumulation. The solicitation does separately address vehicle requirements (section 4.5), but the connection between that practical concern and a formal FAR text-messaging clause is not made explicit.
The Contractor shall adhere to reporting of mishaps in accordance with (IAW) OPNAVINST 5102.1D MCO P5102.1B, Navy & Marine Corps Mishap and Safety Investigation, Reporting, And Record Keeping Manual.
The PWS cites OPNAVINST 5102.1D and MCO P5102.1B — Navy and Marine Corps mishap reporting manuals — without explaining which specific provisions apply to cable technicians performing installation and maintenance work. These are lengthy military instructions designed for military unit safety reporting. While there is a plausible safety rationale for a contractor working on a military installation, the citation provides no guidance on what the contractor is actually expected to do, leaving the vendor to parse lengthy military instructions to determine their obligations.
2.8 Safety. The Contractor shall provide a safe working environment for key consultants and all persons in his/her employ as prescribed by Engineering Manual (EM) 385-1-1, 'General Safety Requirements' and 29 CFR 1910 'Occupational Health and Safety'.
EM 385-1-1 is the Army Corps of Engineers safety manual, primarily designed for construction and major engineering projects. Its citation in a contract for routine cable TV service and internet connectivity — involving technicians connecting cable boxes to coaxial infrastructure — suggests template-driven rather than curated regulatory selection. 29 CFR 1910 (general industry OSHA) is broadly applicable and appropriate, but EM 385-1-1 is a heavy construction-oriented standard that may impose planning, documentation, and safety program requirements disproportionate to the actual hazard profile of cable installation work.
Square Peg
Is the pricing/contract type mismatched to the nature of the work?
Minimal 3 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation is a straightforward match between contract type and requirements. Firm Fixed Price is unambiguously appropriate here. The government is purchasing a defined quantity of commercial cable TV and internet service units (128 total TV units across seven specific locations) from a sole-source regional cable provider. The scope is stable, the deliverables are unambiguous (cable signal and internet connectivity), and the pricing for such commercial utility-like services is well-understood and predictable month-to-month. There is no meaningful labor cost uncertainty, no evolving requirements, and no research or development component that would argue for T&M or cost-reimbursement. The FAR explicitly encourages FFP for commercial items (FAR 12.207), and this is exactly that. The sole-source nature of the acquisition does reduce price competition as a check on cost, but that is a separate concern (a competition issue, not a contract type issue) and does not make FFP the wrong vehicle. No square peg exists here.

This is precisely the kind of stable, recurring commercial service for which FFP is the FAR-preferred vehicle.
