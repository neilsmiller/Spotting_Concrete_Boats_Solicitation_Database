Sin Analysis

Compliance Over Outcomes
Does the contract measure process compliance rather than actual outcomes?
Moderate 3 quotes ▾
Reasoning
The solicitation for F-35 Radar Sustainment Support Follow-On exhibits a moderate compliance-over-outcomes problem. The accountability structure visible in the document relies primarily on Monthly Status Reports that catalog activities, accomplishments, issues, and upcoming milestones — classic process-compliance framing. Deliverables are documentation artifacts (reports, test records, drawings) with acceptance criteria that appear tied to timely delivery of the document rather than to demonstrated system performance. There are no visible performance targets, system availability metrics, mean-time-to-repair standards, or user satisfaction measures in the solicitation text. However, the severity is constrained to a 2 rather than 3 for several reasons. First, the solicitation is relatively lean at 51 pages, and the reporting cadence does not appear to involve the kind of overlapping weekly/monthly/quarterly multi-format pile-on that characterizes the most egregious examples of this sin. There is one primary recurring report (the MSR) rather than a layered stack. Second, the nature of the work — radar sustainment engineering support under a sole-source award for proprietary hardware — does involve tangible deliverables (engineering drawings, test reports) that carry implicit quality criteria even if those criteria are not explicitly quantified in the solicitation. Technical deliverables in this domain are typically evaluated against engineering specifications, which provides some outcome-oriented accountability even if not articulated as SLAs or performance metrics. Third, the solicitation text provided does not include a Quality Assurance Surveillance Plan (QASP), which means the full picture of how the government will evaluate performance is not available — there may be outcome metrics in a QASP or a more detailed PWS attachment that are not captured in the excerpted text. The core problem remains: what is visible in the accountability structure measures process adherence and activity completion, not whether the radar sustainment mission is being accomplished effectively. Given the sole-source context — where competitive discipline is absent and the government depends entirely on its own oversight mechanisms — this gap is more consequential than it would be in a competitive procurement. A well-structured QASP with availability targets or engineering quality metrics would substantially mitigate the concern. Without evidence of that, the solicitation sits at a moderate severity level.

Insider Rewards
Do requirements or criteria favor incumbent or well-connected contractors?
Severe 3 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation represents the most severe manifestation of the Insider Rewards pattern — severity 3 — because competition has been formally eliminated rather than merely discouraged. The solicitation is not simply structured to favor the incumbent; it is explicitly directed to Northrop Grumman under a sole-source authority. A qualified vendor who has never held this contract cannot compete under any circumstances, because the government has invoked FAR 6.302-1 to bypass competition entirely. The stated justification — proprietary data — is particularly significant from a structural standpoint. It indicates that the government does not control the technical data necessary to specify or evaluate this work on a competitive basis. This is a defining characteristic of vendor capture: the contractor retains proprietary rights to data generated during contract performance, which then makes it impossible for the government to solicit competitive proposals for the follow-on. The cycle is self-sealing. While sole-source justifications under FAR 6.302-1 are legally recognized and sometimes genuinely warranted (e.g., for unique technical capabilities or patent-protected systems), their use does not eliminate the policy concern embedded in the Insider Rewards analysis. The question is not whether the award is legal, but whether the structural conditions that made sole-source necessary are themselves the product of prior contracting decisions that failed to preserve competitive options — such as failing to negotiate for data rights, failing to fund government-purpose rights, or allowing the contractor to accumulate proprietary system knowledge without a competitive backstop. The 'Follow-On' framing reinforces that this is a continuing dependency, not a one-time necessity. Content is sufficient for assessment despite the brief notice: the solicitation makes its competitive structure — or lack thereof — fully explicit in the description itself.

This is the most extreme form of insider rewards: the competition is not merely tilted toward the incumbent — it is structurally eliminated. No other vendor can compete regardless of capability, price, or technical approach. The justification ('proprietary data') may reflect genuine vendor capture, where the government cannot even articulate requirements in vendor-neutral terms because the incumbent controls the underlying technical data.

This is a textbook consequence of vendor capture: the incumbent's control of technical data forecloses future competition.
Requirement Sprawl
Does the scope dictate HOW work is done rather than WHAT must be achieved?
Moderate 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation presents a moderate case of requirement sprawl risk, though assessment is constrained by the limited visible content — only 51 pages are attached and the description is brief. The core issue is structural rather than textual: the sole-source justification citing Northrop Grumman's proprietary data is itself strong evidence that requirements are defined in terms of a specific incumbent's processes, tools, and data systems rather than in terms of measurable sustainment outcomes. When proprietary data ownership is the basis for excluding all other vendors, it typically means the SOW describes work that can only be performed using the incumbent's specific methods — which is the definitional root cause of requirement sprawl (requirements written around how the current vendor works rather than what the government needs). The 27-day response window and single-attachment structure suggest a relatively condensed solicitation, which moderately limits the risk of sprawl through sheer volume of prescriptive bullet points. However, the proprietary data rationale introduces a qualitatively different form of sprawl: the solution space is not merely constrained by detailed process mandates, it is effectively collapsed to a single vendor's implementation. This is assessed as severity 2 rather than 3 because: (1) the visible solicitation text is minimal and does not itself contain extensive prescriptive process language; (2) FFP contract type is consistent with outcome-based contracting; and (3) sole-source awards for proprietary technical data in weapons systems sustainment are a recognized and legally authorized procurement pattern — the constraint here is genuine (not organizational dysfunction substituting for outcome definition), even if it forecloses competition. A full severity-3 assessment would require visible SOW language containing step-by-step process mandates, specific staffing structures, or tool prescriptions beyond what proprietary data access inherently requires.

Square Peg
Is the pricing/contract type mismatched to the nature of the work?
Moderate 4 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation uses a Firm Fixed Price requirements-type contract for sole-source F-35 radar sustainment support awarded to Northrop Grumman on a proprietary-data justification. The core tension is between the FFP structure and the sustainment nature of the work. Sustainment contracts — particularly for complex, active weapons systems like the F-35 radar — typically involve demand-driven, reactive workloads whose volume and technical complexity are difficult to predict with the accuracy needed for reliable fixed pricing. This is precisely why T&M or cost-reimbursement contracts are commonly used for depot-level and field sustainment work in defense acquisition. That said, the severity here is moderate (2) rather than severe (3) for several reasons. First, a 'requirements-type' contract under FAR 16.503 does provide meaningful flexibility: it allows the government to order what it needs when it needs it, rather than committing to a fixed scope upfront. This partially mitigates the scope-uncertainty problem. Second, it is plausible that after years of sustainment experience with this system, Northrop Grumman and the government have developed sufficient historical data on labor hours and task types to price sustainment actions with reasonable accuracy — a precondition that would make FFP defensible. Third, the solicitation's 51 pages (not fully reproduced here) may contain CLINs with detailed pricing structures, not-to-exceed ceilings, or other mechanisms that further mitigate the mismatch. The absence of that detail prevents a definitive severity-3 finding. However, the sole-source context is a significant aggravating factor. FFP's efficiency rationale depends on competition keeping prices honest. Without a competing offeror, the 'firm' price is whatever Northrop Grumman and the government negotiate — and the government has limited leverage given the proprietary-data lock-in. This undermines the primary benefit of FFP and arguably makes a cost-reimbursement structure (which provides visibility into actual costs) more appropriate for government accountability. Overall, a moderate (2) finding is warranted: the contract type is defensible but carries meaningful risk of mispricing given the sustainment context and sole-source dynamics.

A 'requirements-type' FFP contract attempts to address variability by ordering against estimated quantities, but the underlying unpredictability of sustainment work remains.
requirements type aquisition
A 'requirements-type' contract (FAR 16.503) orders supplies or services as needed, with no guaranteed minimum beyond stated minimums. While this structure provides some flexibility for variable demand, it does not fully resolve the tension: each task order or delivery under the contract must still be priced as FFP, meaning individual task prices must be estimated upfront even when the scope of specific sustainment actions may be uncertain until the work is actually examined.
Only One Responsible Source and No Other Supplies or Services Will Satisfy Agency Requirements
The sole-source justification means there is no competitive pressure to keep Northrop Grumman's pricing honest. Under FFP in a competitive environment, market forces discipline pricing. In a sole-source scenario, the government is negotiating prices without market comparators, which is a known risk factor for FFP — the 'firm' price may simply reflect whatever Northrop Grumman proposes, undermining the efficiency rationale for FFP.
