Insider Rewards
Do requirements or criteria favor incumbent or well-connected contractors?
Severe 4 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation represents the most severe form of insider rewards, reaching the point where it transcends the typical 'tilted playing field' scenario and becomes a structurally closed competition. The services required are inextricably bound to a specific geostationary satellite (IS-40e) that is owned and operated by SES Space and Defense. No other vendor can physically access the spacecraft to send commands, nor can any other vendor intercept and decode the data downlink — these are not experience gaps that training or hiring could close, but rather exclusive legal and technical access rights tied to asset ownership. The agency correctly invokes FAR 6.103-1 and is at least transparently acknowledging the sole-source nature of the procurement, which is procedurally appropriate. However, this situation is the logical endpoint of the insider rewards cycle: a long-term incumbent relationship has produced a state of total dependency where the government cannot articulate its requirements in vendor-neutral terms because the mission-critical asset (the satellite) is not government property. The four-day response window for capability statements (May 4–8) further underscores that this is largely a procedural notice rather than a genuine competitive inquiry. Severity is 3 — the most severe — because competition is not merely discouraged or disadvantaged; it is structurally foreclosed. A qualified vendor with no prior role on this program cannot compete regardless of technical capability, past performance, price, or any other merit-based factor.
Evidence Quotes
SES Space and Defense owns and operates both the host satellite to which the TEMPO Instrument is integrated and operating on in-orbit, and the ground stations that the commands and data pass through to the host spacecraft.
This statement explicitly identifies why no other vendor can compete: the physical infrastructure (the satellite and ground stations) is owned and operated exclusively by SES Space and Defense. This is not an experience requirement per se, but it reveals that the incumbent possesses unique, non-transferable physical assets that define the entire scope of work — making competition structurally impossible, not merely difficult.
No other company is involved with providing these services as no other company is permitted to send commands to the host spacecraft nor intercept and decode the data downlink.
This is the clearest possible articulation of an absolute insider barrier. The requirement is not about experience, capability, or past performance — it is about exclusive technical and legal access. Only the incumbent can physically perform the work because only the incumbent has the legal authority to command the spacecraft and access the data stream. Any experience requirement layered on top of this would be redundant; the barrier is total.
TEMPO hosted payload services are currently provided by SES Space and Defense using its Intelsat 40e geostationary communications satellite under the United States Space Force contract FA8814-14-D-0003, Delivery Order FA8814-19-F-0001.
The solicitation openly names the incumbent and the prior contract vehicle. The requirement is entirely defined around services already being performed on a specific satellite that is owned by the incumbent. No other vendor could replicate this prior contract history because no other vendor has had any role in this program.
NASA LaRC intends to issue a sole source contract to acquire these services from SES Space and Defense under the authority permitting other than full and open competition implemented by FAR 6.103-1, Only one responsible source and no other supplies or services will satisfy agency requirements.
The agency has formally concluded that the insider rewards dynamic is so complete that it constitutes a sole-source justification. While FAR 6.103-1 may be a valid authority here given the physical constraints, the invocation of this authority confirms that the structure of the requirement — tying services to a specific satellite and ground infrastructure owned by the incumbent — has rendered competition legally and practically impossible.
