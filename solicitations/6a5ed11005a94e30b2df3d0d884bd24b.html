Sin Analysis

Compliance Over Outcomes
Does the contract measure process compliance rather than actual outcomes?
Minimal 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation does not exhibit meaningful Compliance Over Outcomes problems. The scope is a straightforward, bounded professional services engagement — a non-invasive geophysical survey of approximately 2.7 acres across three sites — with a clearly defined deliverable structure: a final report, field documentation (forms, notes, photographs, GPS data, GIS shapefiles), updated CRIS/MDAH site forms, and physical curation of artifacts if generated. These are outcome-oriented deliverables with real-world utility. There is no reporting apparatus of the kind that constitutes this sin: no weekly status reports, no prescribed report formats with mandated sections, no overlapping reporting cadences, and no plan-adherence tracking divorced from actual results. The invoicing structure — COR approval of submittals before payment, itemized by task with percent-complete — is standard and appropriate. The 12-month period of performance with a kick-off meeting and initial schedule is minimal project management overhead for a field survey of this nature. The solicitation does carry significant construction-contract boilerplate (payroll records, weekly WH-347s, daily inspector logs, progress meetings) that appears to have been copied from a construction template and is largely irrelevant to a professional archaeological services contract. However, this represents a document-assembly problem or template mismatch rather than a deliberate compliance-over-outcomes accountability structure. The boilerplate is not operationally meaningful for this scope of work and is unlikely to be enforced as written. The core accountability mechanism — COR acceptance of scientific deliverables before payment — is outcome-focused. The solicitation would benefit from explicit quality criteria for the final report (e.g., what constitutes an acceptable geophysical data set, data density standards, report peer review), but the absence of explicit outcome metrics in a small, specialized CRM survey is not unusual and does not rise to the level of this sin. Overall severity is 1.
Evidence Quotes

Invoices shall include an itemized cost summary of staff work and direct expenses in a level of detail that matches the task agreement proposal, with invoicing based on percent complete for each task and subtask line.
Invoice approval is tied to COR acceptance of submittals, which is a deliverable-based payment gate rather than pure process compliance. Percent-complete invoicing is standard cost-tracking, not outcome measurement, but it functions as a checkpoint tied to actual work products rather than meeting attendance or report generation.

Proposals must include detailed field survey methods, data collection, artifact analysis methods and a curation plan in keeping with NPS and Mississippi Department of Archives and History (MDAH) standards.
The requirement for a pre-approved research design is a process gate, but it is appropriate for this type of scientific survey work where methodology must be vetted before irreversible fieldwork begins. It is not a recurring compliance reporting burden.
Insider Rewards
Do requirements or criteria favor incumbent or well-connected contractors?
Minimal 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation does not exhibit meaningful insider rewards patterns. The experience requirements are capability-focused and domain-appropriate without the stacking mechanisms that characterize this sin. There are no specific dollar thresholds for prior contracts, no minimum contract count requirements, no federal-government-only experience mandates, and no short lookback windows. The requirement to demonstrate 'prior experience and good past performance conducting archeological geophysical surveys at historic sites and cemeteries' is a reasonable capability test — the work involves sensitive cemetery and battlefield sites where specialized geophysical expertise genuinely matters. The personnel qualification standards (Secretary of the Interior's Professional Qualification Standards, MDAH compliance) are industry-wide credentials, not incumbent-specific knowledge. The project is also a relatively small, well-scoped task (2.7 acres across 3 sites) with a clear SOW, and there is no evidence of an entrenched incumbent whose institutional knowledge is embedded in the contract structure. The evaluation criteria mention cost, technical approach, and relevant experience — a balanced set that allows non-incumbents to compete on merits. The solicitation is appropriately set aside for small businesses in the CRM/geophysical survey space, which is a well-populated market of qualified firms. Severity 1 is appropriate.

It references a legitimate domain specialty — archaeological geophysical surveys at historic sites and cemeteries — but imposes no specific dollar thresholds, no minimum number of prior contracts, no federal-government-only mandate, and no short lookback window. The requirement is capability-focused: it tests whether the vendor has relevant technical expertise, not whether they have previously held this specific NPS contract.

This personnel qualification requirement references the Secretary of the Interior's Professional Qualification Standards and Mississippi Department of Archives and History (MDAH) standards. These are established professional credentials applicable to any qualified CRM firm operating in this domain, not incumbent-specific knowledge requirements. A new vendor with appropriately credentialed staff could readily satisfy this requirement.
Requirement Sprawl
Does the scope dictate HOW work is done rather than WHAT must be achieved?
Minimal 2 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation does not exhibit meaningful requirement sprawl. The Statement of Work is outcome-oriented throughout: it defines what NPS needs (a non-invasive geophysical survey of approximately 2.7 acres across three sites, a research design, field documentation, a final report, updated CRIS records) without specifying how the contractor must conduct the survey. The choice of geophysical methods, survey transect spacing, equipment type, data processing approach, and field team structure are all left to the contractor. The core deliverables (research design, field documentation, final report with MDAH submission, bound/unbound and digital copies, artifact curation) are reasonable and standard for this type of professional archaeological services contract. The qualification requirements (Secretary of the Interior's Professional Qualification Standards, MDAH compliance) are regulatory/professional standards, not process prescriptions. The contract clauses document does import a heavy boilerplate construction contract template — complete with payroll records (WH-347), subcontracting SF-1413, weekly payrolls, Buy American construction materials, utility service provisions, and references to a 'Project Inspector's Daily Log' — that is incongruous with a professional services geophysical survey. However, this appears to be a template mismatch (a construction contract template applied to a services contract) rather than genuine requirement sprawl as defined by this sin. The SOW itself, which is the operative scope document, remains appropriately outcome-focused. A vendor reads the SOW and understands clearly what they must deliver, without being told how to deliver it. The template boilerplate is a different structural problem (template misapplication) but does not impose prescriptive how-to requirements on the geophysical survey methodology. Severity is 1 — minimal.
Evidence Quotes
The contractor will design and present a research design and budget estimation to the Parkway for review and approval prior to work.
This is a mild process prescription — it requires a specific sequence (design first, get approval, then work) — but it is a reasonable project management constraint for a sensitive archaeological survey at historic/cemetery sites, not meaningful sprawl. It does not dictate the content of the research design or the methods to be used.
Proposals must include detailed field survey methods, data collection, artifact analysis methods and a curation plan in keeping with NPS and Mississippi Department of Archives and History (MDAH) standards.
This requires the proposal to address certain topics, which could be read as prescriptive, but it actually leaves method selection entirely to the vendor — it says 'in keeping with NPS and MDAH standards,' which are professional standards, not process mandates. Vendors retain full discretion over which geophysical methods (GPR, magnetometry, resistivity, etc.) to propose.
Rule Pools
Does the contract bury contractors in undifferentiated regulatory citations?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation exhibits a moderate-to-elevated rule pool problem rooted in template misapplication rather than deliberate over-regulation. The actual scope of work — a non-invasive archeological geophysical survey of 2.7 acres by professional CRM archeologists — is a professional services engagement. However, the contract vehicle and clause set were drawn almost entirely from a construction contract template (note the SF 1442 form, the construction-format table of contents, and the preponderance of FAR Part 36 clauses). This produces a systematic mismatch: roughly 15-20 of the approximately 40 cited clauses are construction-specific and have questionable or no applicability to the actual work. The Davis-Bacon suite (11 clauses), the construction bonds and performance clauses, the Buy American Act for construction materials (reproduced in full with blank price tables), the lead-based paint prohibition, and the hazardous waste environmental statutes in the safety clause are all artifacts of the construction template. The solicitation provides no contextual explanation for any clause — it is an undifferentiated list. A CRM firm new to NPS contracting would face genuine uncertainty about whether they need to submit weekly certified payrolls, obtain performance bonds, complete SF-1413 forms for subcontractors, and comply with CERCLA and RCRA. That said, the severity is moderated to 2 rather than 3 because: (1) the total clause count, while elevated, is not in the extreme range seen in severe cases; (2) there are no internally contradictory requirements of the type that would force absurd compliance engineering; (3) the SOW itself is well-written, focused, and clearly describes the actual work; and (4) the problem appears to be template laziness rather than deliberate obfuscation. The clause set is a construction boilerplate misapplied to a professional services scope — a common NPS/DOI pattern for small field-work contracts — rather than an accumulation of dozens of agency-specific policy documents without review.

Square Peg
Is the pricing/contract type mismatched to the nature of the work?
Moderate 5 quotes ▾
Reasoning
This solicitation uses a firm-fixed-price contract for a non-invasive archeological geophysical survey of 2.7 acres across three historically documented sites in Mississippi. The FFP structure is generally defensible here: the core deliverable is a bounded, concrete output (geophysical survey data and a final report covering a known acreage at three identified sites), the sites are geographically defined, and the contractor population in the CRM/geophysics space can reasonably estimate costs for this type of work. Non-invasive surveys using instruments like ground-penetrating radar or magnetometry are routine enough that experienced vendors can price them with reasonable confidence. These factors support FFP as an appropriate choice. However, there are moderate concerns. The SOW defers the research design to post-award, meaning the specific survey methods, data collection protocols, and analysis approaches are not locked down at the time of proposal. This is somewhat unusual for an FFP arrangement and introduces methodological scope that vendors must price speculatively. Additionally, fieldwork is explicitly conditioned on weather and soil conditions, introducing scheduling uncertainty that the contractor must absorb in their fixed price. Taken together, these factors could result in either inflated bids (contractors pricing in contingency) or under-bids followed by modification pressure if conditions or the NPS-approved research design prove more demanding than anticipated. That said, the core scope is stable enough — known acreage, known sites, non-invasive methods, defined deliverables (bound and unbound reports, GIS data, site forms) — that FFP is not clearly wrong. The 12-month period of performance provides buffer for weather-related delays. The severity is assessed as 2 (Moderate): the FFP choice is defensible given the bounded deliverable, but the post-award research design development and weather-contingent fieldwork create real scope uncertainty that a different structure (e.g., a labor-hour contract with defined LCATs and a not-to-exceed ceiling) might manage more transparently. Most contracting experts would not call this an egregious mismatch, but would note the tension.

This tangible, discrete deliverable with clear geographic boundaries is a meaningful mitigating factor supporting FFP.

Under FFP, a generous timeline should protect the government, but the combination of undefined research design at award, weather-dependent fieldwork, and a lengthy PoP means the government has limited leverage to drive efficient performance.
